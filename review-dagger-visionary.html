Teacher recoverability in DAgger for VLAs

The project measures how often a large VLA teacher recovers from the states that a small student reaches, and uses this rate to decide which states get the teacher’s labels. The application sends each robot failure to the cheapest expert that can fix it.

Paper: DAgger, arXiv:1011.0686 Role: visionary October 2026 View slides Download presentation

When is a VLA a good DAgger expert?

Proposed research

DAgger’s guarantee has an expert term. Theorem 3.2 gives J(DAgger policy) ≤ J(expert) + u·T·ε + O(1), where T is the horizon and ε is the loss of the best policy in hindsight. The constant u bounds how much the expert’s cost-to-go increases after one mistake of the learner (Section 2.2). The bound is linear in T only when u is small, that is, when the expert recovers from the learner’s mistakes within a few steps.

Recent work uses large VLAs as experts for a small one. VLA-OPD and Multi-Teacher OPD let the student act and label the states that it visits with VLA teachers. Reward-DAgger uses the π0.5 LIBERO checkpoint as its simulated expert, and it kept only the four LIBERO-10 tasks on which this expert “consistently recovers from frequent failure states”. None of them reports how often the teacher recovers from the student’s states. A simulator can measure this directly.

Hypotheses

  1. From states that the student visits, the teacher succeeds less often than from the start of the task. This recovery rate predicts the DAgger gain for each task.
  2. At a fixed label budget, labels only on states that the teacher recovers from give a higher student success than labels on all visited states.
  3. On-policy labels beat offline distillation only when the student is too small to copy the teacher, as the analysis of Foster, Block and Misra suggests. The gap increases as the student gets smaller.

Why it matters. If H1 holds, the labels of a model teacher need a check before use. If H2 holds, the same check also saves labels.

The takeover test

  1. The student runs, and its state s is saved every 10 steps.
  2. From each saved state, the teacher takes over n times. The share of successes is the recovery rate r(s).
  3. From the same state, the student runs again n times. This control shows how often s succeeds without help.

The control matters. In a study of a frozen π0.5 on RoboTwin, 30% of the failed episodes over four tasks succeeded when the same policy ran again. A takeover success alone thus overstates what the teacher adds.

Design

TeacherThe π0.5 LIBERO checkpoint of openpi (96.85% average success on the four LIBERO suites), frozen.
StudentsSmolVLA (450M parameters); π0 with LoRA as a larger student; a diffusion policy as a smaller one.
Round 0Behavior cloning on 10% of the LIBERO demonstrations, so that the students have room to improve.
ConditionsOffline distillation; DAgger with βi = I(i = 1); DAgger with labels only on recoverable states; takeover segments as in HG-DAgger.
TasksLIBERO Spatial, Object, Goal and Long; LIBERO-Plus camera and layout shifts.
MetricsStudent success per teacher label; the distribution of r(s) per task; the correlation of r(s) with the DAgger gain per task; the gap between offline and on-policy labels for each student size.
ControlsThe same teacher-label budget in each condition; 3 DAgger rounds; paired seeds; student re-runs from each tested state.

Compute (estimate). Each round has 2,000 student episodes and takeover tests, with re-runs, on 2,000 saved states. This is about 60 GPU-hours of rollouts, 50 of fine-tuning and 30 of evaluation: 140–180 GPU-hours on one RTX 5090.

Risks. The teacher succeeds on most LIBERO tasks, so few states may be unrecoverable. LIBERO-Long and the LIBERO-Plus shifts give more. The test needs an exact reset to a saved state, which simulators have and real robots do not. FastOPD already distills π0.5 into SmolVLA from the dataset (77–82% against the teacher’s 97.5% on LIBERO), so the contribution is the measurement and the routing, not a new training loss.

Application: a failure router

Proposed application

Recovery data is expensive. A person in VR is the most reliable expert and the slowest one. A policy or a planner with full simulator state is fast, but it can label states that it cannot recover from. The router uses the takeover test to send each failure to the cheapest expert that recovers from it.

  1. A failure state is saved. A policy expert gets the takeover test. If it recovers, it labels the state.
  2. If not, a planner with full state gets the same test. If it recovers, its labels are used.
  3. If both fail, a person in VR corrects the robot.

What a user gets. For long-horizon household tasks: recovery data for each failure type, human time only on the failures that no model recovers from, and a list per task of which expert fixes which failures.

Prior work and gaps. HG-DAgger and RaC use a person for each correction. VLA-OPD uses a model teacher for every state that the student visits. The winner of the 2025 BEHAVIOR Challenge trained on successful demonstrations only, with no recovery demonstrations, and added hand-written correction rules instead.

Pilot (go/no-go). LIBERO-Long, the round-0 SmolVLA student and 300 saved states from failed episodes. The work continues only if the teacher recovers from clearly fewer of these states than its success rate from the start of the task (H1).

Router test. Two or three household tasks in simulation, with a policy expert, a planner with full state and a person in VR. The measures are the student success per expert-minute and the share of failures that each expert recovers from.
