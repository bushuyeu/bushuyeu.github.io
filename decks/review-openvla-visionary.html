The paper

What OpenVLA makes possible

  • +16.5 pointsabsolute success over the closed 55B RT-2-X across 29 tasks on two robots, with 7× fewer parameters.
  • 1.4% of the weightsLoRA at rank 32 came within 1.5 points of full fine-tuning (68.2% vs 69.7%, two Franka tasks), on one A100 instead of eight.
  • 4-bitinference scored like bfloat16 (71.9% vs 71.3%, eight BridgeData tasks) with less than half the memory.
  • 256 bins per dimensionevery action dimension is one token, so every action comes with a probability.

7B: SigLIP and DINOv2 into Llama 2 (Prismatic-7B); 970k Open X-Embodiment episodes; 21,500 A100-hours. Limits it names: one image, ~6 Hz on an RTX 4090, reliability below 90%. The LoRA and 4-bit results use a smaller SigLIP-only variant (footnote 4).

Proposed research

Does cheap fine-tuning break a VLA’s confidence?

Confidence drives help requests

Monitors read token confidence as the chance of success (Zollo and Zemel), train classifiers on it (INSIGHT), conformalize it (ReconVLA) or fit a calibrator on labelled rollouts (VLAConf). Each is fitted to one fine-tuned model: if fine-tuning shifts confidence, raw confidence misleads and learned monitors need refitting.

LoRA makes language models overconfident

Fine-tuning on small datasets does. A post-hoc Laplace posterior over the adapter (Laplace-LoRA) or an ensemble of adapters repairs much of it. For robot policies this is untested: the one VLA calibration study measured released checkpoints, all LoRA at rank 32.

Proposed research

Hypotheses

  1. H1On 10% of the demonstrations, OpenVLA’s confidence exceeds its success by more than on 100%; LoRA more than full fine-tuning at similar success.
  2. H2Bayesian LoRA confidence (Laplace-LoRA, 4 adapters) has lower calibration error than rescaling one adapter’s (temperature, action-wise Platt), on the same rollouts.
  3. H3The gap grows under LIBERO-Plus camera, lighting and layout changes, with calibrators fitted without them.

If H1 holds, raw confidence misleads after a cheap fine-tune and monitors need new labelled rollouts. If H2 holds, the repair is cheap. If neither, post-hoc calibration is enough.

Proposed research

Compute and risks

Compute, estimated

Two suites: 24 runs, 4 of them full. LoRA 10–15 A100-h per run, full 40–120 (8 A100s × 5–15 h): about 360–780 A100-hours, before Laplace fitting and seeds. One LIBERO run is timed first.

Evaluation at 6 Hz (model only, RTX 4090): ~5 GPU-h per checkpoint on Spatial, ~12 on Long, plus simulator time.

Risks

Token-based success confidence is a proxy; the calibration study found it weakest late in failed episodes.

Failures to calibrate against: ~75 of 500 on Spatial, ~230 on Long. An acting ensemble costs 4× the inference.

Proposed application

Rehearse in a digital twin before the robot works

  1. Scan one to three minutes of phone video of the workspace and its objects.
  2. Rebuild it with object meshes, the robot and a matched camera; masses and friction set by hand and varied.
  3. Rehearse each candidate checkpoint 200–300 times on the target tasks.
  4. Calibrate confidence and choose the room’s ask-for-help threshold.
  5. Deploy and check whether the real robot’s caught failures match the twin’s prediction.

What a user gets, and what it builds on

Before the first real trial

  • Which fine-tuned checkpoint to run in this room.
  • The failures to expect, as rollout videos.
  • The confidence threshold for asking for help.

Existing pieces

SIMPLER and ReVeal: simulated and reconstructed success tracks real success. RialTo: twins from quick scans. This routine joins them and adds the calibration step.

Gaps: RialTo needs a person to set objects and physics; ReVeal leaves mass and friction to future work.

Pilot gate: one arm, 2–3 rigid tasks, 3 checkpoints × 30 trials in twin and real. Go only if the twin ranks them the same and is within ~15 points.
