The paper

What OpenVLA makes possible

  • +16.5 pointsabsolute success over the closed 55B RT-2-X across 29 tasks on two robots, with 7× fewer parameters.
  • 1.4% of the weightsLoRA at rank 32 matches full fine-tuning (68.2% vs 69.7%), in 10–15 hours on one A100.
  • 4-bitinference matches bfloat16 in real tasks with less than half the memory.
  • 256 bins per dimensionevery action dimension is one token, so every action comes with a probability.

7B: SigLIP and DINOv2 into Llama 2 (Prismatic-7B); 970k Open X-Embodiment episodes; 21,500 A100-hours. Limits it names: one image, ~6 Hz on an RTX 4090, reliability below 90%.

Proposed research

Does cheap fine-tuning break a VLA’s confidence?

Confidence drives help requests

Recent monitors turn token-based VLAs’ action confidence into help requests (INSIGHT, ReconVLA, VLAConf). They assume it is calibrated: 90% sure means about 90% success.

LoRA makes language models overconfident

Fine-tuning on small datasets does, and Bayesian LoRA repairs much of it (Laplace-LoRA, LoRA ensembles). For robot policies this is untested: the one VLA calibration study measured released checkpoints, all LoRA at rank 32.

Proposed research

Hypotheses

  1. H1OpenVLA becomes more overconfident the fewer demonstrations it is fine-tuned on, and LoRA more than full fine-tuning at equal success.
  2. H2Bayesian LoRA lowers calibration error more than post-hoc fixes (temperature, action-wise Platt scaling) and keeps success the same.
  3. H3The gap grows under LIBERO-Plus camera, lighting and layout changes, where a robot most needs to ask for help.

If H1 holds, every confidence monitor on a LoRA-tuned VLA inherits the error, and the fix is cheap. If not, post-hoc calibration on a small labelled set is enough.

Design

ModelOpenVLA-7B from its released pretrained weights.
Fine-tuningFull; LoRA rank 8; LoRA rank 32 (the default).
Data10% and 100% of each LIBERO suite’s demonstrations.
FixesNone; temperature; action-wise Platt; Laplace-LoRA; 4 LoRA adapters as an ensemble.
TasksLIBERO Spatial, Object, Goal, Long; LIBERO-Plus perturbations for H3.
MetricsSuccess; calibration error, Brier score and failure-prediction AUROC of episode-success confidence.
Trials500 per configuration (10 tasks × 50).

Proposed research

Compute and risks

Compute, estimated

LoRA: 10–15 A100-hours per run, ~60 GB at batch 16. A first pass on two suites is 18 runs: roughly 180–270 A100-hours.

Evaluation: ~6 actions per second, episodes capped at 220–520 steps: at most ~5 GPU-hours per configuration on Spatial, 12 on Long.

Risks

Token-based success confidence is a proxy; the calibration study found it weakest late in failed episodes.

OpenVLA still fails about a quarter of LIBERO episodes (76.5% average): enough failures to calibrate against. An ensemble costs 4× the inference.

Proposed application

Rehearse in a digital twin before the robot works

  1. Scan a few minutes of phone video of the workspace and its objects.
  2. Rebuild it as a reconstruction with object meshes and the robot in a simulator.
  3. Rehearse each candidate checkpoint 200–300 times on the target tasks.
  4. Calibrate confidence and choose the room’s ask-for-help threshold.
  5. Deploy and check whether the real robot’s caught failures match the twin’s prediction.

What a user gets, and what it builds on

Before the first real trial

  • Which fine-tuned checkpoint to run in this room.
  • The failures to expect, as rollout videos.
  • The confidence threshold for asking for help.

Existing pieces

SIMPLER and ReVeal: simulated and reconstructed success tracks real success. RialTo: twins from quick scans. This routine joins them and adds the calibration step.

Risk: lower reconstruction fidelity weakens sim-to-real agreement (ReVeal), so the transfer is what gets measured.
