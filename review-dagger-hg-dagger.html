DAgger and HG-DAgger: paper review

A ten-minute paper review for a VR/XR class: learning from the robot’s own mistakes, human takeover, the evidence, and an experiment on control authority and latency.

DAgger and HG-DAgger

Papers

HG-DAgger: Interactive Imitation Learning with Human Experts. Michael Kelly, Chelsea Sidrane, Katherine Driggs-Campbell and Mykel J. Kochenderfer. Stanford University and University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign. ICRA 2019.

A Reduction of Imitation Learning and Structured Prediction to No-Regret Online Learning. Stéphane Ross, Geoffrey J. Gordon and J. Andrew Bagnell. Carnegie Mellon University. AISTATS 2011.

This paper review covers the problem, the two approaches, novelty, evidence, usable lessons, weaknesses and a proposed VR user study. The Google Slides deck has timed cue notes for a ten-minute talk.

Errors change the states the robot visits

Problem

Behavioral cloning learns to copy expert actions on expert demonstrations. A small mistake at deployment changes the next observation. The learner can then reach states absent from those demonstrations and make further mistakes. DAgger addresses this mismatch by collecting expert labels on states the learner visits.

The training loop and the human gate

Approach

DAgger: roll out the current policy, ask an expert for actions at the visited states, add these examples to the accumulated dataset, retrain and repeat. A mixing probability can initially let the expert execute actions and then decrease.

HG-DAgger: the human chooses when to take over and keeps full control until handing back. Only human-control intervals add new action labels. This gives the human a consistent feedback signal during a correction.

Learning on learner states and estimating risk

Novelty

DAgger connects imitation learning to no-regret online learning. Under its assumptions and repeated collection rounds, it improves the worst-case cost dependence on episode length compared with ordinary cloning. HG-DAgger adds human-gated sampling and a risk estimate based on disagreement among an ensemble of policies. It calibrates a threshold from the final quarter of recorded takeover doubts. The human still chooses the gate during training.

Games and obstacle avoidance

Evidence

In Super Tux Kart, DAgger reached zero falls per lap after 15 training rounds in the reported evaluation (Ross et al., Section 5.1). HG-DAgger was tested in simulated driving and on a physical car navigating simulated obstacles. For the first 5,000 physical-car samples, the paper reports zero collisions and zero road departures for HG-DAgger, compared with one collision and six departures for cloning, and one of each for DAgger (Kelly et al., Table II).

The car result is a small feasibility demonstration. The samples are sequential control samples, not independent driving trials, and the result does not establish general driving safety.

Human control affects the training labels

Lessons and weaknesses

Collecting corrections on learner states changes what the policy can learn. When the expert is a person, control authority also affects the feedback they receive. For XR systems, the takeover control and the visible driving mode become part of data collection.

DAgger requires repeated collection and training, expert labels on difficult states, and a way to manage unsafe rollouts. HG-DAgger assumes the human detects danger soon enough. Its explanation involving perceived lag is not isolated in a controlled user study, its physical evaluation is small, and the risk-threshold rule is a heuristic.

Control authority and latency in VR

Proposed research

Restore the same simulator failures for each interface condition. Counterbalance full takeover versus shared control and low versus added delay across operators. Measure recovery success, time, label consistency and workload, then compare policies trained on the resulting corrections. This is a proposed experiment, not a result reported by either paper.

The separate BEHAVIOR project presentation explains our implemented VR recovery loop and its measured outcomes.
