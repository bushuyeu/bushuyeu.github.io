Harvesting failures for imitation

Human-gated and privileged DAgger in the 2026 BEHAVIOR Challenge. Our team harvested a policy’s failures with simulator snapshots, recovered them in a VR headset and with two automatic experts, had an AI agent and a person review the recordings, and trained on them. This is the short version of the team’s report, with the recordings in place of the report’s stills.

Challenge: BEHAVIOR Challenge 2026 Team report: unpublished draft Hawaiʻi AI Initiative November 2026 View slides Download presentation

Compounding errors and the human gate

Problem

A behavior-cloned policy for a long household task fails when a small error leads to a state the demonstrations never show. Ross et al. show why: with error ε per step on the expert’s states and T steps per episode, the learner’s cost can exceed the expert’s by up to T²ε, because each mistake leads to states where it makes more. Their algorithm, DAgger, labels the states the learner itself visits and retrains on all data so far, which brings the bound down to the order of Tε. The guarantee needs repeated rounds, each harvested with the latest policy.

HG-DAgger makes DAgger usable with a human expert. The learner drives; the human takes over when the state looks unsafe, keeps full control until the robot is back on track, and hands back. Only the takeover segments are labelled. The human expert is also the bottleneck: every recovery needs a person, both to record it and to check it. A simulator with state snapshots makes both ideas concrete, because we can return to any failure the policy produced, as often as we want. On the new tasks we replaced the human expert with scripted controllers and a stronger policy, and moved most of the checking to an AI agent, with a person making the final decision.

The 2026 BEHAVIOR Challenge

Setup

Tasks100 full-length household tasks in OmniGibson: the 50 tasks of the 2025 challenge and 50 new ones, in 7 scenes. The mean demonstration lasts 351 s and passes through 27 skill segments.
RobotR1Pro, a wheeled bimanual robot with a four-joint torso.
InputsRGB and depth from a head camera and two wrist cameras, plus the robot’s proprioception. Privileged simulator state may be used for training but not at evaluation.
ScoreThe Q-score: the share of a task’s goal predicates that hold at the end of the episode, averaged over tasks. Each task has 20 public and 20 hidden test instances.
Data20,000 teleoperated demonstrations (200 per task, 1,950 hours, 3.27 TB in LeRobot format), annotated with 31 skills.
PoliciesAll π0.5 models in one stack, a fork of Team Comet’s 2025 submission. pt50 is Team Comet’s checkpoint for the 50 tasks of 2025. The follower is the team’s instruction-following π0.5 for the new tasks: it executes one phase at a time (“pick up the broom”) and needs a planner that sequences the phases and decides when each is done.
Deadline16 October 2026.

Most results below are phase success rates: the fleet restores the start of a phase from a held-out demonstration, the policy executes that phase alone with a budget of twice the demonstration’s duration, and a privileged detector decides whether the phase goal was reached. Phases are paired across policies, with 95 % bootstrap intervals over tasks.

The recovery pipeline

Method

The human expert and the privileged expert follow the same stages: harvest the learner’s failures, mark them, recover them, record the recovery, and review it before it is published. The third expert, a stronger policy, uses the same harvest and detector.

Human-gated recovery in VR

On the 2025 tasks, pt50 runs unattended on the cluster and saves a simulator snapshot every 30 control steps; every rollout that ends below a perfect score is kept with its video. A reviewer watches each failure at four times speed and marks where it began. An operator in a Meta Quest 3S or Pico 4 Enterprise headset then stands in the scene behind the robot, rendered over NVIDIA CloudXR, and the simulation resumes from the snapshot nearest the mark:

  1. The policy drives, served over the challenge’s websocket protocol.
  2. The operator presses the right controller’s B button to take over. The arms are hot-swapped to inverse-kinematics controllers that follow the operator’s hands; the torso moves along the one direction that holds 97 % of the variance of 427,000 demonstration torso poses.
  3. The operator corrects the robot the way the demonstrations do, and presses B again to hand back. The original controllers are restored.
  4. The console records a 15-second tail of policy driving after the hand-back, and saves the episode.

Each recovery is one LeRobot v2.1 episode with a per-frame intervention mask (1 = human). Human steps are labelled with the joint positions the robot actually reached at the next step, the commanded base velocity and the latched gripper command, so the label does not depend on which controller produced the motion. Control runs at 30 Hz with physics at 120 Hz.

Privileged per-skill recoverers

Human recovery does not scale to thousands of cases. On the new tasks the follower executes one phase at a time, so the harvest changes shape: the fleet restores the start of each phase of each demonstration, lets the follower take it from there, and a privileged phase detector checks whether the phase goal is reached in time. From 8 October, the expert for these failures is a set of scripted recoverers, one per skill, that read the simulator state and solve inverse kinematics on object poses. They cover 11 skills: pick, place, open/close (articulation), hand-over, toggle, pour, sweep, chop, wipe, attach and insert. AI coding agents wrote and iterated them (chop went through nine versions, attach through eight). As in DART, the executed actions are perturbed while the recorded labels stay clean. The detector then confirms that the phase goal is reached: 3,436 of 8,303 failed phases (41 %).

AI review before the human decision

The detector judges only the end state. A recording can pass because the goal holds at the end even if the robot got there badly, for example by cutting an egg while chopping a vegetable. Recordings of the toggle, pour, sweep, chop, wipe, attach and insert recoverers go through a review page: an AI agent running Claude Opus 5.5 watches each one and writes one line on how the robot reached the goal (“Fingertip on burner knob marker, red→green, pan untouched”), marking it “looks right” or “flagged”, and a person then accepts or denies it. Of 1,028 recordings reviewed this way, the agent marked 718 as looking right and flagged 310; the person published 855 and denied 173. The person accepted 704 of the 718 clips the agent called right (98 %) and denied 159 of the 310 it flagged (51 %), so 159 of the 173 denials (92 %) had been flagged first. The agent is a cautious filter: it caught most bad recordings but flagged about as many good ones, which concentrated the person’s attention on 30 % of the clips. Pick, place, hand-over and open/close recordings were published on the detector gate alone.

The strongest 2025 policy as the expert

The third expert is pt50. Many objects and skills of the new tasks also appear in the 2025 tasks, on which pt50 was trained. For a failed pick-up or place phase of the follower, and in a second round for doors, switches and lids, the fleet restores the case snapshot and lets pt50 drive, prompted with the name of a 2025 task that contains the same skill, and the detector keeps the attempts that reach the phase goal. The yield is low, 754 of 4,181 attempts over both rounds, but the actions come from a policy trained on the same kind of human teleoperation as the follower’s demonstrations. The 500 confirmed recordings of the first round, made on 14 October on 35 of the new tasks (459 pick-ups and 41 places), are the recovery data of the submitted follower; a second round on doors, switches and lids added 254 more.

The harvest found failures faster than people could recover them. On the 2025 tasks, pt50 failed 381 of 387 harvested episodes, and in 269 of the failures (71 %) the robot never made progress, so a short correction cannot generalize from them; the addressable pool was about 112 episodes. Recording was slow: 78 human recoveries in the first four days, all on one task. On the new tasks the privileged recoverers reached the phase goal in 3,436 of 8,303 failed phases. Contact-rich skills with a dedicated recoverer recover best, and the skills where the follower fails most recover worst. Each privileged recording is a one-phase clip of about 15 s that starts from the end state of a failed follower attempt; pick-ups are the largest group (1,966), followed by places (760) and toggles (322).

Phase success is measured on held-out phase starts of the new tasks. The d_scr and d_optB_1000 differences are paired against the demonstrations-only control e_demos1k, a 1,000-step fine-tune of the follower with no recovery data, with 95 % bootstrap intervals over tasks; r50 is paired against the follower. The paired phase comparisons were computed by the report’s authors from the team’s raw result files. The short fine-tunes share one recipe: the demonstrations of all 100 tasks plus a share of recovery data, batch 128, peak learning rate 2.5 × 10⁻⁶. The human recoveries of the 2025 task were never trained on: Pilot 1 was cancelled after a cluster staging failure.

The policy expert helped pick-ups. On 383 held-out pick-up phase starts over 49 new tasks, the submitted follower succeeds in 44.6 % against 37.6 % for the demonstrations-only control and 35.9 % for the follower. The control itself is not separable from the follower (+2.1 points, [−2.8, +7.0]), so the gain comes from the corrections, not from the short fine-tune. On a mixed set of all skills the difference shrinks to +1.6 points: 92 % of the corrections are pick-ups, and the gain stays where the data is. Training longer did not help: with the same recipe, 1,000 steps beat 2,000 and 4,000.

The scripted expert hurt. A 10,000-step fine-tune on the privileged recoveries collapsed phase success from 46.5 % to 11.6 %. The report attributes the collapse mainly to the robot’s waist twist, a torso joint that no demonstration moves and that the privileged controllers used freely; pinning the waist at serving time did not restore the policy. The attribution is not clean: the collapse is not separated from learned fumbling or from the long schedule (10,000 steps, against 1,000 for the runs that helped). With the twist zeroed and the same share and schedule as the submitted follower, the privileged data no longer hurt, but it did not help either.

Recoveries as images for the planner

The challenge’s evaluator gives the policy only the task identifier, so the follower needs a planner that turns the task into a sequence of phase prompts and decides when each is done. The privileged recoveries turned out to be useful here, not as actions but as images: each one shows the robot in a state the demonstrations never reach, labelled with the phase it is in. Adding them to each task’s training data raised the share of seconds labelled correctly by a per-task linear phase classifier on SigLIP features, on 198 recorded rollouts of the follower on held-out instances, from 80.8 % (95 % CI [77.4, 84.4]) to 84.4 % ([81.6, 87.2]); paired over the 48 tasks with recoveries, the gain is +3.6 points, and it carried over to a second checkpoint. The submitted planner instead decides when a phase is done with a separate end-of-phase detector, trained on the demonstrations, 3,830 follower rollouts and 4,227 recoveries (4,161 privileged and 66 human). On held-out rollouts it switches within −1 to +5 s of the true completion on 73 % of successful attempts, against 24 % for the previous rule, and switches wrongly on 34 % of stuck attempts, against 64 %. Its records do not isolate the recoveries’ share of this gain.

Closed loop and the submission

Neither effect reaches the end-to-end score yet. With oracle phase labels, the follower completed no task in 394 rollouts of the new tasks. In the closed-loop pilot of 16 October (ten new tasks, public instances 0 and 1, 20 rollouts per arm; arm H stopped at 14), no arm completed a task; arm B, the d_optB_1000 follower with the end-of-phase detector and a timer scaled to each skill’s pace, had the highest numbers (mean Q 0.060, 5 of 20 rollouts above zero) and became the submission. The pilot also showed that the follower is needed on the new tasks: pt50 with mapped prompts scored 0.008. At this level the follower limits the score, not the phase label.

So recovery data reaches the submission in two places: the follower on the new tasks, fine-tuned on pt50’s corrections of its own failures, and the end-of-phase detector. The 2025 tasks run on pt50, which in the team’s test of the submission settings scored a mean Q of 0.155 with 18 successes in 500 rollouts. On the new tasks, 120 rollouts of the final evaluation had finished by 18:00 HST on 16 October: mean Q 0.018, no success. The public leaderboard lists the entry with a Q-score of 0.092 and a success rate of 1.8 % (self-reported, 17 October 2026).

What limited the method

Discussion

  • Prevention, not recovery. The operators resumed before the failure mark, and the simulator is stochastic, so the failure often did not happen again and the operator took over before it would have. Of 33 recordings of the place step, 29 began with the can already in hand. The first batch is prevention data, not recovery data; since 1 October the resume point is 3 s after the mark.
  • Expert time, not failures, was the bottleneck. The harvest produced failures far faster than people could recover them, and 71 % of them could not be fixed by a short intervention. The privileged recoverers removed this bottleneck at the cost of two new ones: a detector that checks only the end state, and a person who has to decide on every recording.
  • A policy’s actions, not a script’s. The two scaled experts started from the same failed states and were gated by the same detector, yet only one helped the policy. pt50’s yield was 18 %, the privileged recoverers’ 41 %, and only pt50’s data moved the policy. Our hypothesis is that pt50’s corrections stay inside the action distribution the follower learned, while the scripts solve each phase their own way. We have not tested this directly.
  • A joint the demonstrations never use. The privileged recoverers reached their goals partly by moving the robot’s waist. Every check in the pipeline passed those recordings: the detector judges the end state, and the AI reviewer and the person judge from the camera views how the robot got there; none compared the recorded actions with the demonstrations. Before a recovery is accepted, its per-joint action distribution should be compared with the demonstrations’, and a joint the demonstrations never move should be a hard reject. A recovery that reaches the goal after fumbling is a success for the detector, but as training data it teaches fumbling.
  • One round, not DAgger. DAgger’s guarantee comes from aggregation over many rounds. We ran at most one round per dataset, so the method as used is targeted data augmentation at failure states. The positive result is a phase success rate measured with a privileged detector, mostly on pick-ups, and it has not been shown to raise the Q-score; the closed-loop evaluations are too small to separate small differences.

Next. A second DAgger round harvested with the improved follower; corrections for the other manipulation skills; and a check of every recovery’s actions against the demonstrations before training.
