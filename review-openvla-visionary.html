When should a fine-tuned OpenVLA ask for help?

OpenVLA made it cheap to adapt a 7B robot policy: LoRA on one GPU matches full fine-tuning. Because OpenVLA writes actions as tokens, it also reports how sure it is of each one. I propose to test whether that cheap fine-tuning keeps the confidence honest, and to set each robot’s ask-for-help threshold in a digital twin of the room where it will work.

Paper: OpenVLA, arXiv:2406.09246 Role: visionary October 2026 Download presentation

What OpenVLA makes possible

The paper

OpenVLA (Kim, Pertsch, Karamcheti et al., 2024) is a 7B vision-language-action model built on Prismatic-7B: SigLIP and DINOv2 image features feeding a Llama 2 language model. It was trained on 970k robot episodes from Open X-Embodiment, in 21,500 A100-hours.

  • +16.5 pointsabsolute success over the closed 55B RT-2-X, across 29 tasks on two robots, with 7× fewer parameters.
  • 1.4% of the weightsLoRA at rank 32 matches full fine-tuning (68.2% against 69.7% on Franka tasks), in 10–15 hours on one A100.
  • 4-bitquantized inference matches bfloat16 in real tasks with less than half the GPU memory.
  • 256 bins per dimensioneach of the 7 action dimensions is written as one token, so every action comes with a probability.

The paper names its own limits: one camera image and no history, about 6 actions per second on an RTX 4090, and reliability below 90%. Two of its strengths meet in this proposal. Fine-tuning is cheap enough that LoRA is the standard way to adapt OpenVLA to a new robot, and its token probabilities are the signal that recent work uses to decide when a robot should ask a person for help.

Research project: does cheap fine-tuning break a VLA’s confidence?

Proposed research

A robot that asks for help must know when it is likely to fail. Token-based VLAs report a confidence for every action, and recent monitors turn it into help requests (INSIGHT, ReconVLA, VLAConf). They assume the confidence is calibrated: when the robot is 90% sure, it succeeds about 90% of the time.

In language models, LoRA fine-tuning on small datasets makes models overconfident, and Bayesian versions of LoRA repair much of it (Laplace-LoRA, LoRA ensembles). For robot policies this is untested. The one calibration study of VLAs (Zollo and Zemel) measured released OpenVLA checkpoints, all fine-tuned the same way (LoRA at rank 32), and did not vary the fine-tuning.

Hypotheses

  1. OpenVLA becomes more overconfident the fewer demonstrations it is fine-tuned on, and LoRA is more overconfident than full fine-tuning at equal success.
  2. Bayesian LoRA (Laplace-LoRA, an ensemble of four LoRA adapters) lowers calibration error more than post-hoc fixes (temperature scaling, the action-wise Platt scaling of the calibration study) and keeps success the same.
  3. The gap grows under the camera, lighting and layout changes of LIBERO-Plus, where a robot most needs to ask for help.

Why it matters. If H1 holds, every confidence-based monitor built on a LoRA-tuned VLA inherits the error, and the fix is cheap: a change in how the adapter is trained, not a new model. If it fails, that is useful too: post-hoc calibration on a small labelled set would be enough.

Design

ModelOpenVLA-7B from its released pretrained weights.
Fine-tuningFull; LoRA at rank 8; LoRA at rank 32, OpenVLA’s default.
Data10% and 100% of each LIBERO suite’s demonstrations.
Uncertainty fixesNone; temperature scaling; action-wise Platt scaling; Laplace-LoRA; an ensemble of four LoRA adapters.
TasksLIBERO Spatial, Object, Goal and Long, OpenVLA’s own fine-tuning benchmark; LIBERO-Plus perturbations for H3.
MetricsSuccess rate; expected calibration error, Brier score and failure-prediction AUROC of the episode-success confidence, measured as in the calibration study; confidence over the course of an episode.
Trials500 per configuration (10 tasks × 50), as in OpenVLA’s LIBERO evaluation.

Compute, estimated. A LoRA run takes 10–15 A100-hours (OpenVLA §5.3) and about 60 GB of GPU memory at batch size 16; full fine-tuning used about 163 GB, sharded across two GPUs (Table 1). A first pass on two suites (Spatial and Long) with three fine-tuning methods, two data sizes and three extra adapters for the ensemble is 18 runs, roughly 180–270 A100-hours. Evaluation runs at about 6 actions per second with episodes capped at 220 steps (Spatial) to 520 (Long), so 500 trials take at most about 5 GPU-hours per configuration on Spatial and 12 on Long. That fits a university allocation; all four suites roughly double it.

Risks. Episode-success confidence built from token probabilities is a proxy, and the calibration study found it weakest late in failed episodes. LIBERO is near saturation for newer models, but OpenVLA still fails about a quarter of its episodes (76.5% average), which leaves enough failures to calibrate against. An ensemble costs four times the inference.

Application: rehearse in a digital twin before the robot works

Proposed application

Calibration needs rollouts with known outcomes from the place where the robot will work, and collecting them on a real robot is slow and sometimes unsafe. A digital twin can supply them. Scan the room with a phone, rebuild it in a physics simulator, and run the fine-tuned OpenVLA there hundreds of times before its first real trial. The outcomes set the room’s ask-for-help threshold and show which failures to fix first.

  1. Scan a few minutes of phone video of the workspace and its objects.
  2. Rebuild it: a Gaussian-splat or mesh reconstruction with object meshes and the robot model in a simulator.
  3. Rehearse: run each candidate fine-tuned checkpoint 200–300 times on the target tasks.
  4. Calibrate: fit the confidence calibrator and choose the room’s ask-for-help threshold.
  5. Deploy and check: on the real robot, compare the share of failures the threshold catches with what the twin predicted.

What a user gets, before the robot’s first real trial in a room:

  • which fine-tuned checkpoint to run there;
  • the failures to expect, as rollout videos to look at;
  • the confidence threshold below which the robot stops and asks for help.

What it builds on. The pieces exist. SIMPLER and ReVeal show that success in simulated and reconstructed scenes tracks real success well enough to rank policies, and RialTo builds twins from quick scans to train policies with reinforcement learning. This application puts them into one routine for deploying a fine-tuned OpenVLA, and adds the step it needs from the research project: setting the room’s ask-for-help threshold from the twin rollouts.

Why it is feasible. RialTo’s interface builds a twin from a quick scan of a real scene; ReVeal’s reconstruction pipelines turn multi-view captures into simulation scenes; OpenVLA already runs in simulators (LIBERO, SIMPLER) and in 4-bit on one GPU.

Impact. A robot in a home, a lab or a classroom that knows where it is likely to fail in that room and asks before it breaks something. The first real trial becomes a check, not a gamble.

The test and its risk. Calibrators fitted on (a) LIBERO, (b) twin rollouts and (c) a few real rollouts are compared on the real robot by calibration error and by the share of failures caught at a fixed number of help requests. ReVeal finds that lower reconstruction fidelity weakens sim-to-real agreement, so the twin calibrator may transfer imperfectly; that transfer is what the experiment measures. Deformable objects and fine contact are out of scope for a first version.
