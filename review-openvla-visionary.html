When should a fine-tuned OpenVLA ask for help?

OpenVLA made it cheap to adapt a 7B robot policy: LoRA on one GPU matches full fine-tuning. Because OpenVLA writes actions as tokens, it also reports how sure it is of each one. I propose to test whether that cheap fine-tuning keeps the confidence honest, and to set each robot’s ask-for-help threshold in a digital twin of the room where it will work.

Paper: OpenVLA, arXiv:2406.09246 Role: visionary October 2026 Download presentation

What OpenVLA makes possible

The paper

OpenVLA (Kim, Pertsch, Karamcheti et al., 2024) is a 7B vision-language-action model built on Prismatic-7B: SigLIP and DINOv2 image features feeding a Llama 2 language model. It was trained on 970k robot episodes from Open X-Embodiment, in 21,500 A100-hours.

  • +16.5 pointsabsolute success over the closed 55B RT-2-X, across 29 tasks on two robots, with 7× fewer parameters.
  • 1.4% of the weightsLoRA at rank 32 came within 1.5 points of full fine-tuning (68.2% against 69.7%, 33 rollouts on two Franka tasks), in 10–15 hours on one A100 instead of 8 A100s for 5–15 hours.
  • 4-bitquantized inference scored like bfloat16 (71.9% against 71.3%, 80 rollouts on eight BridgeData tasks) with less than half the GPU memory.
  • 256 bins per dimensioneach of the 7 action dimensions is written as one token, so every action comes with a probability.

The paper names its own limits: one camera image and no history, about 6 actions per second on an RTX 4090, and reliability below 90%. Its LoRA and 4-bit results come from a smaller OpenVLA variant (a SigLIP-only vision encoder and a smaller data mix, footnote 4 of the paper), on a few tasks, so they show the method works rather than measure the released model. Two of its strengths meet in this proposal. Fine-tuning is cheap enough that LoRA is the standard way to adapt OpenVLA to a new robot, and its token probabilities are the signal that recent work uses to decide when a robot should ask a person for help.

Research project: does cheap fine-tuning break a VLA’s confidence?

Proposed research

A robot that asks for help must know when it is likely to fail. Token-based VLAs give a probability for every action token, and recent monitors build on it in different ways. The simplest reads it directly as the chance of success (Zollo and Zemel). Others train a classifier on token uncertainty (INSIGHT), wrap it in conformal prediction (ReconVLA), or fit a separate calibrator on labelled rollouts (VLAConf). Each is fitted to the rollouts of one fine-tuned model. If the way a model is fine-tuned changes how its confidence relates to success, the raw confidence misleads, and the learned monitors must be refitted after every new fine-tune.

In language models, LoRA fine-tuning on small datasets makes models overconfident. Two Bayesian treatments of LoRA repair much of it: Laplace-LoRA, a post-hoc Laplace approximation over the trained adapter’s weights, and ensembles of separately trained adapters. For robot policies this is untested. The one calibration study of VLAs (Zollo and Zemel) measured released OpenVLA checkpoints, all fine-tuned the same way (LoRA at rank 32), and did not vary the fine-tuning.

Hypotheses

  1. Fine-tuned on 10% of the demonstrations, OpenVLA is more overconfident than on all of them: its mean confidence exceeds its success rate by more. LoRA is more overconfident than full fine-tuning when the two reach similar success.
  2. Bayesian LoRA confidence (Laplace-LoRA, an ensemble of four adapters) has lower calibration error than rescaling one adapter’s confidence (temperature scaling, the action-wise Platt scaling of the calibration study), scored on the same rollouts.
  3. The gap grows under the camera, lighting and layout changes of LIBERO-Plus, where a robot most needs to ask for help, when the calibrators fitted without those changes are used unchanged.

Why it matters. If H1 holds, raw confidence misleads after a cheap fine-tune, and a monitor fitted to one fine-tune needs new labelled rollouts for the next. If H2 holds, the repair is cheap: a posterior over the adapter or a few more adapters, not a new model. If both fail, post-hoc calibration on a small labelled set is enough, which is also useful to know.

Design

ModelOpenVLA-7B from its released pretrained weights.
Fine-tuningFull; LoRA at rank 8; LoRA at rank 32, OpenVLA’s default. The same step budget and tuning effort for each.
Data10% and 100% of each LIBERO suite’s demonstrations; the 10% subset is drawn per task. Two sizes test a contrast, not a trend; a 30% point is added if the budget allows.
ConfidenceRaw token confidence; temperature scaling; action-wise Platt scaling; Laplace-LoRA on the rank-32 adapter; an ensemble of four rank-32 adapters. All are scored on the same rollouts of one acting adapter, so success stays fixed. Running the ensemble as the policy is a separate comparison with its own rollouts.
MatchingFor “similar success” in H1, LoRA and full fine-tuning checkpoints are matched on success measured with a different simulator seed, never on the test rollouts.
TasksLIBERO Spatial and Long first, then Object and Goal (OpenVLA’s own fine-tuning benchmark); LIBERO-Plus perturbations for H3.
MetricsSuccess rate; mean confidence minus success (the direction of the error); expected calibration error, Brier score and NLL of the first-step confidence, as in the calibration study; failure-prediction AUROC, added here; reliability diagrams; confidence over the course of an episode.
Trials500 rollouts per configuration (10 tasks × 50 initial states, one seed), as in the calibration study; OpenVLA’s paper averages three seeds, 1,500 rollouts. 95% bootstrap intervals.
SplitsFitted fixes and thresholds use episode-level 20% / 80% calibration and test splits, repeated 1,000 times, as in the calibration study. Test episodes are never used for fitting or for choosing a method.

Compute, estimated. The first pass covers two suites (Spatial and Long), three fine-tuning methods and two data sizes: 12 runs. The ensembles add three rank-32 adapters per suite and data size: 12 more, 24 runs in all, 4 of them full fine-tuning. OpenVLA reports, on its own Franka datasets, 10–15 A100-hours for a LoRA run (one GPU, about 60 GB at batch size 16) and 8 A100s for 5–15 hours for a full fine-tune (163 GB at batch size 16, sharded across two GPUs; §5.3, Table 1). At those rates the 20 LoRA runs take about 200–300 A100-hours and the 4 full runs 160–480: 360–780 in all, before Laplace fitting and repeat seeds. The first weeks time one LIBERO run before the grid is fixed. Evaluation: at OpenVLA’s 6 actions per second (model only, on an RTX 4090), 500 episodes capped at 220 steps (Spatial) or 520 (Long) take up to about 5 or 12 GPU-hours per checkpoint, plus simulator time; the 12 checkpoints need about 100 GPU-hours. Full fine-tuning needs 80 GB GPUs; adding Object and Goal roughly doubles everything.

Risks. Episode-success confidence built from token probabilities is a proxy, and the calibration study found it weakest late in failed episodes. LIBERO is near saturation for newer models, but OpenVLA still fails often enough to calibrate against: about 75 of 500 Spatial episodes and 230 of 500 Long episodes at its published 84.7% and 53.7%. An ensemble costs four times the inference when it acts.

Application: rehearse in a digital twin before the robot works

Proposed application

Calibration needs rollouts with known outcomes from the place where the robot will work, and collecting them on a real robot is slow and sometimes unsafe. A digital twin can supply them. Scan the room with a phone, rebuild it in a physics simulator, and run the fine-tuned OpenVLA there hundreds of times before its first real trial. The outcomes set the room’s ask-for-help threshold and show which failures to fix first.

  1. Scan one to three minutes of phone video of the workspace and its objects.
  2. Rebuild it: a Gaussian-splat or mesh reconstruction with object meshes, the robot model and a camera where the real one is. Collision shapes, masses and friction are set by hand and varied at random, since today’s reconstructions do not recover them.
  3. Rehearse: run each candidate fine-tuned checkpoint 200–300 times on the target tasks.
  4. Calibrate: fit the confidence calibrator and choose the room’s ask-for-help threshold.
  5. Deploy and check: on the real robot, compare the share of failures the threshold catches with what the twin predicted.

What a user gets, before the robot’s first real trial in a room:

  • which fine-tuned checkpoint to run there;
  • the failures to expect, as rollout videos to look at;
  • the confidence threshold below which the robot stops and asks for help.

What it builds on. The pieces exist. SIMPLER and ReVeal show that success in simulated and reconstructed scenes tracks real success well enough to rank policies, and RialTo builds twins from quick scans to train policies with reinforcement learning. This application puts them into one routine for deploying a fine-tuned OpenVLA, and adds the step it needs from the research project: setting the room’s ask-for-help threshold from the twin rollouts.

Why it is feasible, and what is still missing. OpenVLA already runs in simulators (LIBERO, SIMPLER) and in 4-bit on one GPU. ReVeal rebuilds workspaces from one to three minutes of phone video. RialTo builds a twin from a quick scan, but a person separates the objects and sets joints and physical parameters, and its policies use point clouds, not OpenVLA’s camera images. ReVeal leaves object-level fidelity, mass and friction to future work. So the twin is engineering with known gaps, and a pilot comes first.

The pilot, a go/no-go gate. One arm OpenVLA already supports (for example the WidowX of BridgeData, which SIMPLER also simulates), one tabletop and two or three rigid-object tasks with clear success checks. Three checkpoints run 30 times each in the twin and on the real robot, from matched start positions. The routine continues only if the twin ranks the checkpoints in the same order as the real robot and its success rates are within about 15 points of the real ones.

Impact. A robot in a home, a lab or a classroom that knows where it is likely to fail in that room and asks before it breaks something. The first real trial becomes a check, not a gamble.

The test and its risk. Calibrators fitted on (a) LIBERO, (b) twin rollouts and (c) a few real rollouts are compared on held-out real rollouts by calibration error and by the share of failures caught at a fixed number of help requests. ReVeal finds that lower reconstruction fidelity weakens sim-to-real agreement, so the twin calibrator may transfer imperfectly; that transfer is what the experiment measures. Deformable objects and fine contact are out of scope for a first version.
