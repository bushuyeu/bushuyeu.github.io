Confidence calibration of fine-tuned OpenVLA

The project tests whether the fine-tuning method and the amount of data change how well the action-token confidence predicts success, and whether a calibrator still works after a new fine-tune. The application tests the robot many times in a simulated copy of its workspace to decide when it should ask for help.

Paper: OpenVLA, arXiv:2406.09246 Role: visionary October 2026 View slides Download presentation

Does fine-tuning change calibration?

Proposed research

Token-based VLAs such as OpenVLA give a probability for each action token. Help-request monitors read this signal directly (Zollo and Zemel), train a classifier on it (INSIGHT), conformalize it (ReconVLA), learn a value estimate from it (TD calibration) or fit a separate calibrator (VLAConf). Each monitor is fitted to the rollouts of one fine-tuned model. TD calibration includes OpenVLA on LIBERO-10, but it does not change the fine-tuning. Römer et al. compare an ensemble and a last-layer Laplace approximation during the fine-tuning of flow-based VLAs, and the ensemble tracks success better. In language models, LoRA on small datasets causes overconfidence, and Bayesian LoRA reduces it (Laplace-LoRA, LoRA ensembles).

I found no controlled comparison of full fine-tuning and LoRA, or of data budgets, for the success calibration of OpenVLA. Zollo and Zemel measured only released checkpoints, all trained with LoRA at rank 32. The practical question: after a new fine-tune, does the old calibrator still work, and how many new labels are needed?

Hypotheses

  1. With a fixed number of training updates, 10% of the demonstrations gives a larger gap between mean confidence and success than 100%. At matched success, the gap of LoRA is larger than the gap of full fine-tuning.
  2. A calibrator fitted on one fine-tune and used again on another predicts success worse than a calibrator refitted with the same number of new labels.
  3. For a fixed acting adapter, an ensemble and Laplace-LoRA, tested separately, give a lower held-out NLL than the best simple calibrator.
  4. Under a mild LIBERO-Plus shift, the gap of LoRA grows more than the gap of full fine-tuning, with calibrators frozen from unshifted data.

Why it matters. If H1 and H2 hold, each new fine-tune needs new labeled rollouts, and the learning curve of H2 says how many. If H3 holds, the adapter brings its own repair.

Design

Stages1: LoRA at rank 32 on 10% and 100% of one suite, three training replicates each. 2: full fine-tuning on the same subsets, after the compute is confirmed. 3: the ensemble and Laplace-LoRA, then the shift. Rank 8 and more suites come only after stage 1.
TrainingA fixed number of updates per run, so a 10% run sees each demonstration about ten times as often. Nested subsets, sampled per task. The same image preprocessing and action normalization in all runs.
ConfidenceThe first-step score of Zollo and Zemel. Simple calibrators: the success prior, Platt scaling and action-wise Platt scaling. Then an ensemble of rank-32 adapters and Laplace-LoRA, which score the tokens of one acting adapter.
Evaluation500 test rollouts per checkpoint (10 tasks × 50 initial states). Matching and checkpoint selection use separate validation start states. Each run saves the first-step logits, the token IDs and a manifest, and each comparison runs on one hardware setup.
MetricsHeld-out NLL and the signed gap first. Then the Brier score, ECE, failure AUROC and the failures caught at a fixed help rate.
SplitsTask-balanced 20% / 80% calibration and test splits, repeated 1,000 times, with a learning curve over the calibration-set size. Method comparisons use the same splits, in pairs.
PowerAbout 75 failures in 500 Spatial episodes give a standard error of about 0.036 on the AUROC, so differences under 0.1 are not detectable. The training replicates, not the splits, measure the training variance.

Compute (estimate). Stage 1 has 6 LoRA runs, about 60–90 A100-hours at the rates of OpenVLA (10–15 per LoRA run, §5.2–5.3), plus about 30 GPU-hours of evaluation. One run is timed first. Stage 2 has 6 full fine-tuning runs, 240–720 A100-hours (40–120 per run), on GPUs with 80 GB or more. It starts only when the allocation is confirmed.

Risks. Token-based success confidence is a proxy, and Zollo and Zemel found it weakest late in failed episodes. LIBERO-Plus can collapse OpenVLA: success falls to 1.1% under camera changes and to 4.4% under lighting changes, from 76.5% without changes. Thus the shift for H4 must be mild, and separate pilot data sets it. The same episode gave a different first-step confidence on two GPU setups (0.81 on one RTX 5090, 0.89 on two A4000), so hardware stays fixed within a comparison.

First result: modest overconfidence, weak failure discrimination

Preliminary result

I reproduced the baseline of Zollo and Zemel on the released LIBERO-Spatial checkpoint. The run used 500 episodes (10 tasks × 50 initial states, one seed) and the settings of the OpenVLA evaluation script. It ran on one RTX 5090 at 7 steps per second, in 2 h 40 min. The confidence is the first-step score of Zollo and Zemel, the mean over the 7 action dimensions of the highest action-token probability. The result is a compatible reproduction of their baseline. It does not yet test H1–H4.

  • 85.2%success (s.e. 1.6 points). OpenVLA reports 84.7%.
  • ECE 0.06195% interval 0.048–0.100. Zollo and Zemel report 0.088.
  • +4.7 pointsmean confidence (0.899) minus success, 95% interval 1.7–7.8.
  • AUROC 0.57failure prediction, 95% interval 0.50–0.65.

Interpretation. Over all tasks the model is slightly overconfident (+4.7 points). Per task, the gap goes from −9 to +21 points. In the first 60 steps, the confidence ranks the episodes that fail only slightly better than chance. The ECE alone does not show this, so the design adds the signed gap, the AUROC and the failures caught. Over the whole episode the AUROC is 0.83, but failed episodes run longer and can include evidence from after the failure, so this is not an early warning.

A constant predictor sets the bar

I fitted simple calibrators to the same rollouts on 1,000 task-balanced 20% / 80% calibration and test splits. A constant equal to the success rate of the calibration episodes gives a lower held-out NLL than the raw confidence. With about 15 failures in a calibration set, the slope of Platt scaling sometimes changes sign, and action-wise Platt scaling overfits. A threshold frozen on the calibration episodes flags 10.7% of the test episodes and catches 16% of the failures. The accepted episodes still fail 13.9% of the time, against 14.8% for all episodes. A new score must beat the constant.

Application: rehearsal in a digital twin

Proposed application

Calibration needs labeled rollouts from the workspace where the robot will operate. Real rollouts are slow to collect and can be unsafe. A digital twin of the workspace can supply them before the first real trial. The aim is fewer failures for a given number of help requests.

  1. Scan the workspace and its objects with one to three minutes of phone video.
  2. Rebuild the scene in a simulator with object meshes, the robot model and a matched camera. Set collision shapes, masses and friction by hand, and randomize them.
  3. Rehearse each candidate checkpoint 200–300 times on the target tasks.
  4. Calibrate the confidence and set the help threshold.
  5. Deploy on the real robot, and compare the failures caught with the prediction of the twin.

The user gets a checkpoint choice, videos of the expected failures and a help threshold for the workspace.

Prior work and gaps. SIMPLER and ReVeal show that success rates in simulated and reconstructed scenes rank policies as real ones do. ReVeal rebuilds workspaces from phone video but does not recover object fidelity, mass or friction. RialTo needs a person to separate the objects and set the physical parameters, and its policies use point clouds, not camera images. The twin is thus engineering with known gaps.

Pilot (smoke test). One arm that OpenVLA supports (for example the WidowX of BridgeData), one tabletop and two or three rigid-object tasks. Three checkpoints run 30 times each in the twin and on the robot from matched start positions. 30 trials give a success rate only to about ±18 points, so the pilot checks the pipeline, not the transfer.

Transfer test. The twin calibrator and its threshold are frozen. Separate real rollouts then score them against a calibrator fitted only on real rollouts and one fitted on twin and real rollouts, with the same number of real labels. The measures are the held-out NLL, the signed gap and the failures caught at a fixed help rate, each with an interval. The test thus shows how many real labels the twin saves.

The test uses the first-step confidence (before the task) and the per-step confidence (during the task). The robot logs each help request but does not stop, so a request does not change the outcome. A monotone rescaling does not change which episodes are flagged, so the ranking measures use raw scores.

Risk. ReVeal finds that lower reconstruction fidelity weakens sim-to-real agreement, and it measures success rates, not confidence. The transfer of the twin calibrator is what the test measures. Deformable objects and fine contact are out of scope.

How the two parts connect

One loop

  1. Fine-tune with LoRA or Bayesian LoRA (research project).
  2. Rehearse in a twin rebuilt from phone video (application).
  3. Calibrate and set the help threshold (research project).
  4. Deploy, then act or ask for help (application).
  5. Correct. A person takes over, and the corrections become new fine-tuning data for step 1.
