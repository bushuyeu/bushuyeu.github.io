When should a fine-tuned OpenVLA ask for help?

The project tests whether the fine-tuning method changes how well the action-token confidence predicts success. The application tests the robot many times in a simulated copy of its workspace to decide when it should ask for help.

Paper: OpenVLA, arXiv:2406.09246 Role: visionary October 2026 Download presentation

Does fine-tuning change calibration?

Proposed research

Token-based VLAs such as OpenVLA give a probability for each action token. Help-request monitors use this signal directly (Zollo and Zemel), through a learned classifier (INSIGHT), with conformal prediction (ReconVLA) or through a separate calibrator (VLAConf). Each monitor is fitted to one fine-tuned model. In language models, LoRA fine-tuning on small datasets causes overconfidence, and Bayesian LoRA reduces it (Laplace-LoRA, LoRA ensembles). I found no controlled test of the fine-tuning method or the data size for VLA calibration. Zollo and Zemel measured only released OpenVLA checkpoints, all trained with LoRA at rank 32.

Hypotheses

  1. Fine-tuning on 10% of the demonstrations gives a larger gap between mean confidence and success than fine-tuning on 100%. At similar success, LoRA gives a larger gap than full fine-tuning.
  2. Bayesian LoRA (Laplace-LoRA, an ensemble of four adapters) gives a lower calibration error than temperature or action-wise Platt scaling of one adapter.
  3. Under the camera, lighting and layout changes of LIBERO-Plus, the gap between LoRA and full fine-tuning increases when the calibrators stay frozen.

Why it matters. If H1 holds, a monitor fitted to one fine-tuned model needs new labeled rollouts after each new fine-tune. If H2 holds, a posterior over the adapter or a few more adapters repairs the confidence without a new model.

Design

ModelOpenVLA-7B, released pretrained weights.
Fine-tuningFull, LoRA at rank 8, LoRA at rank 32 (the default). Each has the same step budget and tuning effort.
Data10% (sampled per task) and 100% of the demonstrations of each suite.
ConfidenceRaw, temperature scaling, action-wise Platt scaling, Laplace-LoRA, an ensemble of four rank-32 adapters. All are scored on the rollouts of one acting adapter.
TasksLIBERO Spatial and Long, then Object and Goal. H3 uses LIBERO-Plus at one severity on the rank-32 LoRA and full checkpoints.
MatchingH1 compares LoRA and full checkpoints at success matched on a different simulator seed.
MetricsSuccess, mean confidence minus success, and the ECE, Brier score and NLL of the first-step confidence, as in Zollo and Zemel. The design adds failure AUROC and reliability diagrams.
Trials500 rollouts per configuration (10 tasks × 50 initial states, one seed). OpenVLA reports the mean of three seeds.
SplitsEpisode-level 20% / 80% calibration and test splits, repeated 1,000 times, as in Zollo and Zemel. A 20% Spatial split holds about 15 failures, so a learning curve over the calibration-set size tests whether this is enough.

Compute (estimate). The first pass has 24 runs on Spatial and Long. Of these, 12 form the grid and 12 are extra ensemble adapters, and 4 are full fine-tuning. OpenVLA reports 10–15 A100-hours per LoRA run and 40–120 per full run on Franka data (§5.2–5.3). At these rates, the first pass needs 360–780 A100-hours before Laplace fitting and repeat seeds. Full fine-tuning needs GPUs with 80 GB or more. Evaluation needs about 200 GPU-hours, and the first result below gives the measured rate.

Risks. Token-based success confidence is a proxy, and Zollo and Zemel found it weakest late in failed episodes. OpenVLA fails about 75 of 500 Spatial episodes and 230 of 500 Long episodes, which is enough to calibrate against. An acting ensemble costs four times the inference.

First result: calibrated on average, weak at predicting failure

Preliminary result

I reproduced the baseline of Zollo and Zemel on the released LIBERO-Spatial checkpoint. The run used 500 episodes (10 tasks × 50 initial states, one seed) and the settings of the OpenVLA evaluation script. It ran on one RTX 5090 at 7 steps per second, in 2 h 40 min. The confidence is the first-step score of Zollo and Zemel, the mean over the 7 action dimensions of the highest action-token probability.

  • 85.2%success (s.e. 1.6 points). OpenVLA reports 84.7%.
  • ECE 0.06195% interval 0.048–0.100. Zollo and Zemel report 0.088.
  • +4.7 pointsmean confidence (0.899) minus success.
  • AUROC 0.57failure prediction, 95% interval 0.50–0.65.

Interpretation. The confidence is calibrated on average, but in the first 60 steps it ranks episodes only slightly better than chance. Thus the ECE alone does not show the problem, and the design adds the signed gap, the AUROC and the warning time. Over the whole episode the AUROC is 0.83, but only because failed episodes continue after the successful ones end. For the application, a threshold on the raw first-step confidence catches few failures on this suite.

Limits. One suite, one seed and one checkpoint. Success per task is 68–98%. LIBERO-Long, where Zollo and Zemel report an ECE of 0.381, is next.

Application: rehearsal in a digital twin

Proposed application

Calibration needs labeled rollouts from the workspace where the robot will operate. Real rollouts are slow to collect and can be unsafe. A digital twin of the workspace can supply them before the first real trial. The aim is fewer failures for a given number of help requests.

  1. Scan the workspace and its objects with one to three minutes of phone video.
  2. Rebuild the scene in a simulator with object meshes, the robot model and a matched camera. Set collision shapes, masses and friction by hand, and randomize them.
  3. Rehearse each candidate checkpoint 200–300 times on the target tasks.
  4. Calibrate the confidence and set the help threshold.
  5. Deploy on the real robot, and compare the failures caught with the prediction of the twin.

The user gets a checkpoint choice, videos of the expected failures and a help threshold for the workspace.

Prior work and gaps. SIMPLER and ReVeal show that success rates in simulated and reconstructed scenes rank policies as real ones do. ReVeal rebuilds workspaces from phone video but does not recover object fidelity, mass or friction. RialTo needs a person to separate the objects and set the physical parameters, and its policies use point clouds, not camera images. The twin is thus engineering with known gaps.

Pilot (go/no-go). One arm that OpenVLA supports (for example the WidowX of BridgeData), one tabletop and two or three rigid-object tasks. Three checkpoints run 30 times each in the twin and on the robot from matched start positions. The work continues only if the twin ranks the checkpoints in the real order. Its success rates must also be within about 15 points of the real ones.

Transfer test. Calibrators fitted on (a) LIBERO, (b) twin rollouts and (c) a few real rollouts are frozen together with the twin threshold. They are then scored on unseen real rollouts from matched start positions. The measures are the calibration error and the signed gap. At a fixed number of help requests, it also counts caught failures, false alarms, missed failures and the warning time before an unrecoverable error.

The test uses the first-step confidence (before the task) and the per-step confidence (during the task). The robot logs each help request but does not stop, so a request does not change the outcome. A monotone rescaling does not change which episodes are flagged, so the ranking measures use raw scores.

Risk. ReVeal finds that lower reconstruction fidelity weakens sim-to-real agreement, and it measures success rates, not confidence. The transfer of the twin calibrator is what the test measures. Deformable objects and fine contact are out of scope.

How the two parts connect

One loop

  1. Fine-tune with LoRA or Bayesian LoRA (research project).
  2. Rehearse in a twin rebuilt from phone video (application).
  3. Calibrate and set the help threshold (research project).
  4. Deploy, then act or ask for help (application).
  5. Correct. A person takes over, and the corrections become new fine-tuning data for step 1.
